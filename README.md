# BMS workbench

**A browser-based workbench for biomedical science practicals. It brings together a virtual microscope with slides of human tissues, the anatomy of the human body in 3D, a chart recorder with real recordings of people, an organ bath for the pharmacology of isolated tissues, flow diagrams of physiological control that run as simulations, an electrical circuit simulator that also speaks the language of physiology (pressure, flow, resistance and compliance), interactive simulations from PhET, exam practice that works like an online exam system, a 3D viewer for proteins and other molecules, and RStudio, with the instructions for the practical alongside them.**

Everything runs in the student's web browser. Students don't need to install anything, there is no server-side code, and students' files, answers and essays are stored in their own browser. Because the site is static (just HTML, CSS, JavaScript and the data: slides, models, recordings and simulations), it can be hosted for free on **GitHub Pages** or on any ordinary web server.

The workbench is intended as the base for the course's practicals, in the same way as the R, bioinformatics and pedigree workbenches. A week's practical might ask students to write an essay in *Exam practice*, then look at slides in the *Microscope*, measure and count, and analyse their measurements in *RStudio*: the tools link together, and each practical uses this page with its own chapters in the left-hand pane (see [Writing a practical](#writing-a-practical)). The workbench comes with a short starter practical, *A tour of the workbench*, which shows each tool without using the course's own teaching material.

## What's in the workbench

The workbench is a single page, `index.html`. The **instructions** are on the left: chapters made up of steps that tick themselves off as the student completes them, and questions with model answers (some of which check a numerical answer). The **workbench** itself is on the right, with eleven tabs. Students can hide the instructions (with the button at the top left, or <kbd>Ctrl</kbd>+<kbd>\</kbd>) so that the workbench fills the screen. On a phone or a tablet, the workbench opens on top of the instructions (the **Workbench** button), and ✕ at the right of its tabs goes back to them. When there isn't room for all the tabs' names, they are set in smaller type, and when even that doesn't fit, the tabs show only their icons and the open tab keeps its name (on the smallest phones, the open tab is shown by its colour). The page's **Help** shows which icon is which tab.

| Tab | What it contains |
| --- | --- |
| **Microscope** | A virtual light microscope. The **slide box** holds 31 slides, sorted by the system of the body: 29 sections of human tissue stained with H&E, from the GTEx project (normal tissues, except a coronary artery with atherosclerosis), and two slides drawn for practising measurement (a stage micrometer and a haemocytometer with living and dead cells). **Objectives** from 1× to 40× (keys 1 to 6) follow the convention of digital microscopy – 20× shows a slide at the full resolution of its scan, 0.5 µm per screen pixel – with a scale bar, a readout of the magnification and the field of view, and a map of the whole slide. The **eyepiece view** shows a round field of view with a graticule of 100 divisions, which students calibrate against the stage micrometer. The tools **measure** lengths and areas, **count** cells in up to six categories, and add **labels**; the camera takes **pictures** with a scale bar and the slide's credit, which keep their scale when they are opened again. Many slides carry teaching **labels** (hidden at first, so that students look for themselves), and the instructions can ask students to **find** a labelled structure and click on it (*Show me* takes them to an example and rings it). Every measurement and count is kept in `~/microscope/measurements.csv` and `~/microscope/counts.csv`, ready for R, and the microscope opens a ready-made R script for them. Students can also open a picture of their own (a photograph through a microscope, say) as a slide, and give it a scale from its scale bar. |
| **Recorder** | A chart recorder for real recordings of people, from the open-access databases of PhysioNet: 14 excerpts – the ECG (sinus rhythm, a 12-lead ECG, ventricular ectopic beats, atrial fibrillation, second-degree heart block), heart sounds with the ECG, blood pressure on standing up, the pulse (PPG) with breathing, heart rate and breathing (respiratory sinus arrhythmia), heart rate during exercise, needle EMG (healthy, myopathy, neuropathy) and the EEG with the eyes open and closed. Stacked channels on one time axis, zoom and scroll (and an overview of the whole recording), **Play** to replay a recording in real time as if it were being recorded (with sound for the heart sounds and the EMG), two-cursor **calipers** (Δt, the rate 60 ÷ Δt, the change and the lowest, highest and mean value of a channel), a marker, a **heart-rate channel** worked out beat by beat, the beat labels and rhythms given by cardiologists, a **data pad** of measurements kept as `~/recorder/measurements.csv`, CSV export of a recording (and its beats) for R, scripts for R, and pictures. |
| **Organ bath** | Isolated-tissue pharmacology, simulated: a length of guinea-pig ileum or a ring of rat aorta (with or without its endothelium) in an organ bath, drawn with its transducer, aeration and water jacket. Students add drugs by volume from stock solutions (the bath concentration is worked out exactly, C<sub>stock</sub> × *v* / (*V* + *v*), and cumulative doses add up), watch the tension on a chart-recorder trace with the doses marked under it, wash out, and let time run at ×1, ×5 or ×20 (or skip to the plateau, or wait 10 minutes for an antagonist to equilibrate). Each response is measured automatically into a **Results** table – kept as `~/organbath/<tissue>.csv` – and **Analyse in R** writes and opens a script that fits Hill curves with `nls()`, works out EC<sub>50</sub>s, dose ratios and pA<sub>2</sub>, and draws a Schild plot. Agonists, a partial agonist, competitive, non-competitive and irreversible antagonists, an anticholinesterase, nicotine and a ganglion blocker, nitric oxide and its synthase, KCl, and two unknown drugs to identify. The responses come from the operational model of agonism with noise, kinetics and differences between tissues ([build/organbath/README.md](build/organbath/README.md)): it is a teaching simulation, not data from real tissues. |
| **Diagrams** | Flow diagrams of physiological control: boxes for the quantities, stimuli, set points and comparators, and arrows that stimulate (+) or inhibit (−), each with a strength and a delay. **Run** turns the diagram into a simple model and plots how each quantity changes with time, so that students can see what negative and positive feedback, feed-forward and delays do. Boxes can be knocked out or held fixed. Diagrams are saved in `~/diagrams` (`.flow.json`) and can be exported as pictures (PNG or SVG) and their results as a table for R (CSV). Examples: feedback in temperature control, feed-forward, positive feedback in clotting, a delayed loop that oscillates, blood glucose, and a template for an endocrine axis. |
| **Circuits** | An electronics bench: batteries, current sources, AC sources, resistors, capacitors, inductors, switches, diodes, voltmeters and ammeters, joined by wires, with a simulator that works out the voltages and currents as time passes, and traces that plot them. In **Physiology** mode the same parts become pressure and flow sources, resistances to flow, compliances, valves, pressure gauges and flowmeters (in mmHg and mL/s). Examples include Ohm's law, a voltage divider, resistances in series and in parallel, charging a capacitor, the membrane as an RC circuit, the parallel-conductance model of the resting membrane potential and the Windkessel model of the circulation. Circuits are saved in `~/circuits` (`.circuit.json`), and can be exported as pictures and their traces as CSV. |
| **Simulations** | A library of 14 **PhET Interactive Simulations** (University of Colorado Boulder) chosen for biomedical science – Neuron, Membrane Transport, Diffusion, Gas Properties, Under Pressure, pH Scale, Acid-Base Solutions, Molarity, Beer's Law Lab, Gene Expression Essentials, Color Vision, Geometric Optics, Capacitor Lab: Basics and Least-Squares Regression – kept with the site in the versions PhET published under CC BY 4.0, and run in the tab with PhET's usage statistics switched off. Search and topics, each simulation's screens, full screen, and an About box with what it is for, its authors, its version and its licence. |
| **Exam practice** | Essay writing as in the online exam system (Inspera), so that students can get used to writing in a browser before they have to: a start page, a timer, one question at a time with an essay editor (paragraph styles, bold, italic, underline, sub- and superscript, lists, indents, tables, special characters, formulas, drawings, undo and backups), a word count with a limit, flags, question tiles, a calculator, resources (which can open slides in the microscope), a submission page, a receipt, and a review with the marking guidance. As in Inspera's exam browser, only what was copied in the exam can be pasted into it: text copied from anywhere else is refused. Answers save themselves as students type (in `~/essays`), and a submission can be downloaded as a **Word document**, as a PDF (by printing), as a web page or as text. Papers are JSON files (`exams/`), and *Write an essay* starts a free essay for any title. |
| **Anatomy** | The human body in 3D, from **Z-Anatomy** (CC BY-SA 4.0; most of its models made from BodyParts3D): 12 systems (skeleton, muscles, heart and great vessels, arteries and veins, respiratory, digestive, urinary, male reproductive, endocrine glands, brain, spinal cord with nerves and eyes, lymphoid organs) and 2216 structures, each with its English and Latin names (and TA2 number) and, for about half of them, the opening of its Wikipedia article. Turn the body round and zoom; show, hide or fade each system; click a structure to select it, then isolate it, fade the others round it or hide it; standard views (anterior, posterior, left, right, superior, inferior); sagittal, coronal and transverse sections with a slider; labels in the manner of an atlas; *find* tasks ("Find the thyroid gland and click on it"); pictures saved in Files (`~/anatomy`). Converted from Z-Anatomy's Blender file by `build/anatomy` (12 glTF files, 9 MB in all, each downloaded only when its system is first shown) and drawn with three.js. |
| **Molecules** | The structures of proteins and other molecules in 3D, drawn by NGL: cartoons, sticks, spheres and surfaces, colour schemes, selections, binding sites and their contacts, labels, distances and pictures. A **library** of 17 proteins of physiology (haemoglobin, myoglobin, insulin, a G-protein-coupled receptor with its G protein, steroid receptors, transporters, pumps and channels, and others) and 18 small molecules (hormones, transmitters, glucose, ATP, cAMP, haem…) is kept with the site; other entries are loaded from the Protein Data Bank or AlphaFold DB. |
| **RStudio** | An IDE modelled on RStudio, running R 4.6 in the browser (webR): scripts, R Markdown and Quarto, the Console, Environment, Plots, Packages, Help and Viewer panes, *Import Dataset*, and Shiny apps. The microscope's measurements and counts, the recorder's measurements and recordings, the organ bath's results, the results of diagrams and the traces of circuits are all CSV files that R reads. |
| **Files** | The student's home folder (`~`, which is `/home/user`): the folder tree (with upload, download – a folder downloads as a `.zip` file – and new, rename, move and delete), an editor, and viewers for tables, pictures, PDFs and web pages. Buttons above each file send it where it belongs: a diagram to Diagrams, a circuit to Circuits, a picture to the Microscope, an R script to RStudio, a structure to Molecules. |

**The tools work together.** The microscope's pictures, measurements and counts go to Files and on to R, and so do the recorder's measurements and the organ bath's results (each with an R script that analyses them); a run of a diagram or a circuit can be kept as a CSV file; an exam paper can open slides in the microscope; and the buttons in the instructions open slides, parts of the body, recordings, tissues, simulations, diagrams, circuits, papers and molecules, choose tools and run code.

## The starter practical

*A tour of the workbench* takes about 3 hours 40 minutes and has 12 chapters. It shows each tool with generic examples (the kidney, the liver, a stage micrometer, a haemocytometer, the endocrine glands, the ECG, acetylcholine on the ileum, haemoglobin…), not the course's lectures, so it can be given to students at the start of the course, or used as a model for writing practicals.

| Chapter | Tab | What students do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0. Start here (10 min) | Microscope | Find out what each tab is for and how the page works |
| 1. The microscope (25 min) | Microscope | Go from low power to high power on the kidney; find a renal corpuscle; look at proximal tubules with the help of the slide's labels; add a label of their own and take a picture; find a portal tract and a central vein in the liver |
| 2. Measuring and counting (30 min) | Microscope | Calibrate the eyepiece graticule against a stage micrometer at 10× and 40×; measure renal corpuscles; work out magnifications and real sizes from scale bars; count living and dead cells in a haemocytometer and work out cells per mL and viability |

| 4. 3D molecules (10 min) | Molecules | Look at haemoglobin, its chains and its haem groups; open small molecules from the library |
| 5. Recordings (20 min) | Recorder | Measure R–R, PR and QT intervals and the heart rate on a real ECG; compare ventricular ectopic beats and atrial fibrillation; watch the heart rate rise and fall with breathing; find the alpha rhythm of the EEG; analyse the data pad in R |
| 6. The organ bath (25 min) | Organ bath | Set up a guinea-pig ileum; work out bath concentrations from stocks and volumes; build a concentration–response curve for acetylcholine with wash-outs; repeat it in atropine and see the parallel shift; fit the curves in R and work out the dose ratio and pA<sub>2</sub> |
| 7. Diagrams (15 min) | Diagrams | Run an example, change it and run it again; draw a diagram of their own, run it and keep it |

| 9. Simulations (15 min) | Simulations | Stimulate a neuron and watch the action potential (Neuron); test the Beer–Lambert law (Beer's Law Lab); read the H⁺ concentration of blood on the logarithmic pH scale (pH Scale) |

The model answers are included in the page (students see them with *Show answer* under each question; see `showModelAnswers` below), so the questions are meant for practice rather than assessment.

## Before students use it

1. **Computers and browsers.** Students need a laptop or desktop computer with a current version of Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari; 8 GB of memory is comfortable. The workbench also works on a phone, although the screen is cramped (the simulations are best with the phone turned sideways). The Anatomy and Molecules tabs need WebGL (3D graphics), which almost every computer has.
2. **Network.** Students' browsers need to reach your site (the page and the slides) and, for the tabs that use them: `webr.r-wasm.org` (R itself, about 30 MB the first time RStudio is opened, then kept in the browser's cache) and `repo.r-wasm.org` (R packages, when they are installed); `models.rcsb.org` and `files.rcsb.org` (structures from the Protein Data Bank that are not in the library) and `alphafold.ebi.ac.uk` (AlphaFold models). The Microscope, Anatomy, Recorder, Organ bath, Diagrams, Circuits, Simulations, Exam practice and the library of the Molecules tab only need your site.
3. **Shared computers.** Students' files and essays are stored in the browser profile, so computers that wipe profiles when students log out will lose them. Tell students to download their work before they leave (*My files → Download all my files*, *My answers → Download my answers*, and *Download* on each submitted essay), and to use *Start afresh* when they finish on a shared computer.
4. **Exam practice is practice.** It works like the candidate's view of an online exam system, but it is not one: it is not locked down, it doesn't send anything to a server, and the student can see the model answers after submitting. Use it for practice and formative work, not for assessed exams. Whether the browser's spelling checker is on at the start is set for each paper (it is off by default, as it usually is in exams); students can switch it.
5. **Personal and patient data.** The slides are public, de-identified research images. If you add your own slides or pictures, make sure that you may publish them (anything on the site is public).

## Publishing it on GitHub Pages

1. On GitHub, open the repository's *Settings → Pages → Build and deployment* page. Under Source choose **Deploy from a branch**, then choose the branch **main** and the folder **/ (root)**, and click Save. (The repository needs to be public, unless your GitHub plan supports Pages for private repositories.)
2. After a minute or two, the site will be available at `https://<owner>.github.io/<repository>/`, which for this repository is `https://comparativechrono.github.io/BMS-workbench/`. That is the address to give to students.
3. The first time a browser opens the site, the page reloads itself once. This installs a small service worker (`isolation-sw.js`, next to `index.html`), which makes the page *cross-origin isolated*, so that R can wait for typed input and be interrupted with <kbd>Esc</kbd>; it also serves the Shiny apps that R runs.

**Size and bandwidth.** The site is about 420 MB: the slides take 360 MB (`slides/`, about 12 MB each), the simulations 37 MB (`sims/`, 1–6 MB each), the anatomy models 10 MB and the recordings 2 MB. Each is downloaded only when a student opens it. GitHub Pages allows a published site of up to 1 GB and has a soft limit of 100 GB of traffic a month. A student who looks at a slide only downloads the parts they look at, at the magnifications they use (the microscope asks for each tile of the slide with an HTTP *range request* into the slide's `tiles.pack` file, which GitHub Pages and most web servers answer), so a practical with a few slides usually costs a few megabytes per student. If you add many more slides, keep an eye on the size of the repository (GitHub recommends staying under 1 GB) or host the slides elsewhere (see `slideBase` below).

The empty file `.nojekyll` stops GitHub from processing the site. The site itself doesn't need the `build/` and `test/` folders, but do keep `assets/vendor/licenses/`. To host the workbench on a different web server, just copy the folder (you can leave out `build/` and `test/`); the server must answer range requests (almost all do), and the address must use `https` (or be `localhost`) for the service worker to work.

**Students' files belong to the site's address.** The browser stores them for that address, so if the workbench moves to a different address, students will start with an empty home folder there. All the GitHub Pages sites of one account share the same address (`https://<owner>.github.io`), which matters when you have more than one practical: see `storePrefix` below.

## Trying it on your own computer

Opening `index.html` directly from your disk (`file://…`) doesn't work, because browsers block the Web Workers and the data files. Instead, serve the folder with a local web server:

```bash
cd BMS-workbench
python3 serve.py 8000        # then open http://localhost:8000
```

`serve.py` answers range requests (which the microscope needs) and sends the cross-origin isolation headers itself. Python's own `python3 -m http.server` does **not** answer range requests, so the microscope only works with it after downloading each slide in full.

## Settings in `config.js`

`assets/js/config.js` is the only file you need to edit for most changes.

| Setting | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| `courseTitle`, `courseSubtitle` | The name shown at the top of the page (and in the browser tab), and the line underneath it |
| `hostname` | The computer name shown after the user's name (`user@workbench`) where the workbench shows it |
| `storePrefix` | The prefix for everything the page saves in the browser (files, answers, essays and settings). Sites at the same address, such as all the GitHub Pages sites of one account, share their files if their `storePrefix` is the same (see [Writing a practical](#writing-a-practical)) |
| `showModelAnswers` | When this is `true`, there is a *Show answer* button under each question. Set it to `false` to hide the model answers (they are still in the page's source code, and adding `?answers` to the address shows them) |
| `recordingBase` | The folder (or web address of a folder) that holds the Recorder's recordings; another site can hold them, as long as it allows requests from this page (CORS) |
| `slideBase` | The folder (or web address of a folder) that holds the slides. Another site can hold them, as long as it allows requests from this page (CORS) and answers range requests |
| `objectives`, `micronsPerScreenPixelAt20x` | The microscope's objectives (1, 2, 4, 10, 20 and 40) and the scale of its magnifications: at 20×, one screen pixel shows 0.5 µm of the slide, so a slide scanned at 20× is shown pixel for pixel. The calibration of the eyepiece graticule follows from these (with its 100 divisions of 4 screen pixels, `DIV_PX` in `scope.js`): one division is 4 µm at 10× and 1 µm at 40×, as the starter practical's answers say, so change them together |
| `examBase`, `spellcheckDefault`, `autosaveSeconds` | Where the papers of exam practice are; whether the browser's spelling checker is on when a free essay starts; how often an answer that is being typed is saved |
| `examplesBase` | Where the examples of Diagrams and Circuits are |
| `webrBase`, `rRepos` | Where R is downloaded from (webR 0.6.0), and the repositories that R packages are installed from |
| `pdbExample`, `afExample` | What the Molecules tab's boxes suggest |
| `storageWarnMB` | How many megabytes of files a student can store before the page warns them |

## Writing a practical

### Making a new practical from the workbench

For each new practical, make a copy of this repository. The easiest way is to tick *Settings → General → Template repository* on this repository once, and then use *Use this template → Create a new repository* for each practical. Then, in the copy, change:

- `build/chapters.py`, which contains the chapters (see below);
- the files in `starter/`, which every student finds in their home folder on their first visit (`starter/data/x.csv` becomes `~/data/x.csv`); files that a student already has are left alone. If you add files after students have started, increase `STARTERS_VERSION` in `build/make_page.py` so that they receive the new files;
- the slides, papers and examples the practical needs (see [Slides, models, recordings, simulations, papers and examples](#slides-models-recordings-simulations-papers-and-examples)). A practical doesn't need every slide: you can delete the folders of the slides it doesn't use, to make the site smaller;
- `courseTitle`, `courseSubtitle` and `storePrefix` in `assets/js/config.js`;
- in `build/template.html`, the `<title>`, the description, and the `data-page` attribute of `<body>`, which is the name under which the page saves its ticks, answers and settings.

Then run `python3 build/make_page.py`, try the page with `python3 serve.py`, and commit the new `index.html` together with your other changes.

**Sharing files between practicals.** If two practicals are hosted at the same address (for example, two GitHub Pages sites of one account) and have the same `storePrefix`, they share the student's home folder, so measurements, pictures and essays made in one practical are available in the next. Giving them different `data-page` values keeps their ticks and answers separate. If a practical's files must be kept apart from the others, give it its own `storePrefix`. Note that *Start afresh* in one practical deletes the files of every practical that shares its `storePrefix`.

**A week's practical** might be, for example: an essay in Exam practice (a paper of its own in `exams/`, opened with `exam('week3-essay')`), then a histology section in which students find structures (`scopefind('thyroid-gland:follicle')`), measure them (`scopetool('measure:follicle')`) and take pictures, and a short R chapter that analyses the measurements. The starter practical shows each of these pieces.

### Writing the chapters

The chapters are written in Python, in `build/chapters.py`, using the helper functions in `build/lib.py`. Running `python3 build/make_page.py` puts them into `index.html` (between the `<!--CHAPTERS-->` and `<!--/CHAPTERS-->` markers in `build/template.html`), and also writes the lists that the tabs read (`slides/index.json`, `exams/index.json` and `examples/index.json`). It reports an error (and exits with an error status, after writing the page) if two tasks, questions or headings have the same name. `chapters.ALL()` lists the chapters in order. A chapter can also be kept in a file of its own, `build/ch_<name>.py`, with a function `chapter(num)` that returns it, as the chapters of the Anatomy, Recorder, Organ bath and Simulations tabs are (inside such a file, call the helper as `lib.chapter(...)`, since the file's own function is called `chapter` too).

| Helper | What it produces |
| --- | --- |
| `chapter(id, num, title, tab, heading, minutes, lede, body)` | A chapter. `num` and `title` appear in the chapter bar at the top, `tab` is the workbench tab shown with the chapter (`scope`, `anatomy`, `viewer` – Molecules –, `recorder`, `bath`, `flow`, `circuit`, `sims`, `exam`, `rstudio` or `editor`), and `heading`, `minutes` and `lede` give its heading, how long it takes and its opening sentence |
| `activity(title, [task(...), ...])` | A numbered list of steps |
| `task(id, html, auto='…', check='…')` | A step with a tick box. It ticks itself when an event on the page matches `auto`, or when the condition named by `check` is true |
| `q(id, question, model_answer, accept=, hint=, placeholder=)` | A question with a box for the student's answer and a model answer. If `accept` is given, the answer is checked automatically: regular expressions separated by `\|\|`, or a **number** – `num:4±0.2`, `num:1.145e6±4%` (a tolerance), `num:57..63` (a range) or `num:400`. Numbers can be typed as `1.2e6`, `1.2 × 10^6`, `1.2×10⁶` or `1,200,000`, with units after them |
| `mcq(id, question, [(option, correct, why), ...])` | A multiple-choice question |
| `callout(kind, title, html)` | A highlighted box: `info`, `tip`, `warn` or `concept` |
| `scope(spec)` | A button that opens a slide: `'kidney-cortex'`, or `'kidney-cortex@1.4,2.95,10x'` (centred 1.4 mm from the left and 2.95 mm from the top of the slide, at 10×) |
| `scopefind('slide:key')` | A button that asks the student to find a labelled structure on a slide and click on it (*Show me* helps) |
| `scopetool(name)` | A button that chooses a tool of the microscope: `measure`, `area`, `count`, `label`, `capture` (take a picture), `eyepiece:on`/`eyepiece:off`, `labels`/`labels:off`. `measure:renal corpuscle` also labels the measurements that follow, and `count:Living cells,Dead cells` names the categories to count |
| `scoper('measurements')`, `scoper('counts')` | A button that opens the microscope's R script for the measurements or counts in RStudio |
| `flow(ref)`, `flowrun()` | Buttons that open a diagram (`'example:delay-oscillation'` or `'~/diagrams/my.flow.json'`) and run it |
| `circuit(ref)`, `circuitrun()` | The same for circuits (`'example:rc-circuit'`) |
| `exam(paper)`, `calc()` | Buttons that open a paper of exam practice (its id, or `'free'` for a free essay), or the calculator |
| `anat(spec)`, `anatfind(spec)` | Buttons that show something in the Anatomy tab – a system (`'endocrine'`), a structure (`'endocrine:thyroid-gland'`), several systems (`'respiratory+skeleton'`), a view (`'heart@posterior'`), a section (`'section:transverse@0.6'`), labels (`'endocrine+labels'`) – or ask the student to find a structure and click on it (`'suprarenal-gland'`; `'a\|b'` accepts either). ## Slides, models, recordings, simulations, papers and examples

**Slides** live in `slides/<id>/` (an image pyramid of WebP tiles packed into one file, with `slide.json` describing it); `build/make_page.py` lists them in `slides/index.json`, sorted by system. [build/slides/README.md](build/slides/README.md) explains how the GTEx slides were chosen and made, how to label a slide for *find* tasks, and how to add a slide of your own (`build/slides/add_image.py`, from a photograph with a known scale or from a slide scanner's file).

**The models of the Anatomy tab** are in `anatomy/`: one glTF file for each part of the body (the skeleton, the muscles, the heart, the vessels…), `index.json`, which lists the 2216 structures (their ids, English and Latin names, TA2 numbers, systems and groups), and `definitions.json`. They were made from Z-Anatomy's Blender file by the scripts in `build/anatomy/` ([build/anatomy/README.md](build/anatomy/README.md) explains how, what was simplified and what was left out, and why); `build/anatomy/config.json` says which structures go into which file. A *find* task or a *show me* button names a structure by its id in `index.json`.

**The recordings of the Recorder** are in `recordings/`: for each, a `.json` file (its channels, units, sampling rate, annotations, where it comes from and how to cite it) and a `.bin` file (the samples, as 16-bit numbers), listed in `recordings/index.json`. They are excerpts of PhysioNet's open-access databases, made by `build/recordings/make_recordings.py` from the list in `build/recordings/recordings.json` (the script checks each download against the database's checksums); [build/recordings/README.md](build/recordings/README.md) gives each recording's source, licence, citation and what was changed. To add a recording, add an entry to `recordings.json` (only from a database whose licence allows it) and run the script. `recordingBase` in `config.js` lets another site hold them.

**The simulations** are in `sims/`, one folder for each (PhET's file, unchanged, and a picture), listed in `sims/index.json`, which `build/sims/fetch_sims.py` writes from `build/sims/sims.json`. Each is pinned to the last version PhET published under CC BY 4.0: **don't replace a simulation with PhET's latest version**, which is licensed CC BY-NC (see [build/sims/README.md](build/sims/README.md)). To add a simulation, add it to `sims.json` with the version to use, and run `fetch_sims.py`, which refuses a file that doesn't carry the CC BY 4.0 licence.

**The organ bath** has no data files: its tissues, drugs and their values are in `assets/js/organbath.js`, and [build/organbath/README.md](build/organbath/README.md) gives the model, the values and where they come from.

**Papers of exam practice** are JSON files in `exams/` (the format is described at the top of `assets/js/exam.js`): a title, the time allowed, instructions, resources (the calculator, slides, links) and questions – information pages, essays (with a word limit, which can be a hard limit), short answers, numerical questions (with units and a tolerance) and multiple-choice questions – each with marking guidance that students see when they review their submitted answers. Pasting is kept within the exam unless a paper says `"clipboard": "any"` (as an exam taken in an ordinary browser allows). The workbench comes with three: *Getting to know exam practice*, *Practice essay (45 minutes)* and *Calculations*. Write a new paper for each essay you set, and open it from the practical with `exam('its-id')`.

**Examples of diagrams and circuits** are the `.flow.json` and `.circuit.json` files in `examples/diagrams/` and `examples/circuits/`, offered in each tab's Examples menu. The easiest way to make one is to build it in the tab, save it, download it from Files, and copy it into the folder (give it a `title`, a `description`, a `group` and an `order`).

**The Molecules tab's library** is in `molecules/` (BinaryCIF files of the proteins, SDF files of the small molecules), and the list is in `assets/js/viewer-bms.js`. [molecules/README.md](molecules/README.md) says where they came from and how to add one.

## What students keep

Students' files, ticks, answers, essays and settings are stored in the browser's storage for the site (IndexedDB and local storage) on their own computer, and they are still there when the student comes back in the same browser. Nothing is stored on a server, and nobody else can see them. A different browser, a different computer or a private window won't have them, and clearing the browser's data for the site deletes them. *My files → Download all my files* downloads everything as a `.zip` file, *My answers → Download my answers* downloads the answers as a web page, and each essay can be downloaded as a Word document. The page warns students if it is open in two tabs of the same browser, because both would write to the same files.

## Privacy

The site sets no cookies and has no tracking or analytics. The only things that leave a student's computer are requests for the page and its slides, models, recordings and simulations (from your server); R and its packages (from `webr.r-wasm.org` and `repo.r-wasm.org`), when RStudio is used; structures that are not in the library (from RCSB PDB and AlphaFold DB), when the student asks for them; and anything the student's own R code requests. Essays are never sent anywhere. Shiny apps run inside the page. The PhET simulations are opened with PhET's usage statistics switched off (`?yotta=false`), so they contact neither PhET nor Google.

## Browser support and limitations

The workbench works in current versions of Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Safari. It was tested in Chromium; please try the browsers your students use, particularly Safari.

- **The slides** were scanned with a 20× objective (about 0.49 µm per pixel), so 40× enlarges the scan without showing finer detail, and fine structures that need oil immersion (bacteria, the fine structure of chromosomes) can't be seen. The slides are regions of a few millimetres cut from each GTEx section, not the whole section. They are sections of normal tissue (apart from the coronary artery, which has atherosclerosis), mostly from donors after death, so some show the changes of autolysis or of age (the slide's *About* panel gives the GTEx pathologist's notes).
- **The Anatomy tab**'s body is a man's (BodyParts3D was made from one man), so there are no female reproductive organs. The ear, the inside of the kidney and the cerebral white matter are left out because of their licences, and the teeth, joints, skin, meninges, pleura and peritoneum to keep the models small and clear. About half the structures have a definition. A section shows the inner surfaces of the organs it cuts (the cut faces are not filled), and the thinnest nerves and vessels are easier to find with the search box than by clicking.
- **The recordings** are short excerpts (13 seconds to 10 minutes) of PhysioNet's databases, with the annotations those databases give. In four of them (the 12-lead ECG, the heart sounds, the blood pressure on standing and the pulse), the beats were found by a detector when the recordings were made and checked by eye, not by a cardiologist (their *About* panels say so). The ECG during exercise and the heart sounds are in arbitrary units (their databases give no calibration), and the EEG's eyes-open and eyes-closed parts are two recordings put side by side (the Recorder marks the join). Sound plays at the chosen speed, so ×2 raises its pitch.
- **The organ bath** is a teaching simulation: its responses come from a model with typical, rounded values (with noise and differences between tissues), not from real tissues, and only nicotine desensitises. It has two preparations (the guinea-pig ileum and the rat aorta), and the unknown drugs are for the ileum only.
- **The simulations** are PhET's versions from before 29 March 2026 (the last under CC BY 4.0), so they won't receive PhET's later fixes; they are the English versions only. PhET designs them for a wide screen, so on a phone they are best turned sideways. A few steps of the starter practical tick themselves from values inside the simulations, which a new version could rename (the tests check them).
- **Exam practice** imitates the candidate's view of an online exam system; the real system may differ in its details, and it changes from year to year. It runs entirely in the browser: there is no lockdown, invigilation or plagiarism check (the page refuses pastes and drops of text copied elsewhere, but nothing stops a student retyping it), the clock is the computer's own, and the answers and marking guidance of a paper are in its file in `exams/` (so a determined student can read them). Formulas go into Word documents as text with sub- and superscripts, not as equation objects, and the spelling checker is the browser's own.
- **R in the browser** is R 4.6 (webR 0.6.0): one thread, a few gigabytes of memory, packages from the webR repository (most of CRAN, including ggplot2 and dplyr), and documents knitted to HTML only. RStudio's tab is an imitation made for teaching.
- **Cross-origin isolation.** R can only wait for typed input and be interrupted with <kbd>Esc</kbd> if the page is cross-origin isolated. GitHub Pages can't send the headers for this, so `assets/js/isolation.js` registers `isolation-sw.js`, a service worker that adds them (it doesn't cache anything). On a student's first visit, the page reloads itself once. Adding `?isolation=off` to the address removes the service worker.
