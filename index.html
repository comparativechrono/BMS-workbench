What’s in the workbench

The workbench on the right brings together the tools of a biomedical science practical. Each tab is a different tool:

MicroscopeA virtual microscope with slides of human tissues: objectives from 1× to 40×, an eyepiece graticule, and tools to measure, count, label and take pictures.
AnatomyThe human body in 3D, system by system: turn it round, pick out structures, isolate them and cut sections through them.
MoleculesProteins and smaller molecules in 3D, with a library of molecules of physiology.
RecorderReal recordings of people – the ECG, heart sounds, blood pressure, breathing, muscle and the brain – to play, measure and analyse as on a chart recorder.
Organ bathAn isolated tissue in an organ bath: add drugs, watch it contract or relax, and build concentration–response curves.
DiagramsFlow diagrams of how the body controls itself – boxes, and arrows that increase (+) or decrease (−) – which you can run to see how things change with time.
CircuitsElectrical circuits, and the circuit models of physiology, built and run as on a lab bench, with meters and traces.
SimulationsInteractive simulations from PhET of the science behind physiology: neurons, membranes, pH, solutions, light and more.
Exam practiceAn essay editor that works like the online exam system: a timer, a word count, a calculator, flags, and downloads as Word documents.
RStudioR, for analysing data – including the measurements and counts that you make with the other tools.
FilesEverything you save: measurements, counts, pictures, recordings, results, diagrams, circuits, essays and R scripts.

The tools work together. The microscope, the recorder and the organ bath keep your measurements and results in files that R can read, and your pictures in Files; you can run a diagram or a circuit and keep its results; and the essays you write in Exam practice can be downloaded as Word documents. This short practical takes you round each of the tools, so that you know your way around before you use them in the practicals of the course.

Looking at a slide

≈ 25 min

The Microscope tab works like a light microscope: a slide box, objectives from 1× to 40× and a stage you move by dragging. In this chapter you look at the kidney and the liver, find structures, label them and keep pictures.

Its objectives work as they do in digital microscopy: at 20× you see a slide at the full resolution of its scan, and 10× shows it half as big, 40× twice as big. The slides are sections of human tissues (normal, apart from one coronary artery with atherosclerosis), stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and scanned at high resolution, so you can look at a whole section at low power and then zoom in on single cells. Haematoxylin stains nuclei blue-purple and eosin stains cytoplasm and collagen pink.

From low power to high power

Always start with a low-power objective, which shows you the whole section, so that you can see how the tissue is organised and choose where to look. Then move up through the objectives. The readout at the bottom right of the view shows the magnification and the size of the field of view, and the map at the top right shows which part of the slide you are looking at.

Look at the kidney
  1. Open the slide of the kidney . You are looking at the outer part of the kidney, the cortex.
  2. Click on the name of the slide, above the view, to read about it: how it was stained, how big it is, what to look for, and where it comes from.
  3. Click the 10× objective (or press 4, as the objectives are numbered 1 to 6). Drag the slide to move round it, and scroll (or pinch) to zoom in and out.
  4. Now look at part of the slide with the 40× objective (6).
Magnification, resolution and the field of view

Through a real microscope, the total magnification is that of the objective multiplied by that of the eyepiece (usually 10×): a 40× objective gives 400×. As the magnification goes up, the field of view gets smaller: at 40× you see a quarter of the width of the field at 10×. (On a screen, how much you see also depends on the size of your window.) What limits the detail you can see is the resolution, the smallest distance between two points that can still be told apart. These slides were scanned with a 20× objective, about 0.5 µm per pixel, so the 40× objective makes the picture bigger without showing more detail. A real microscope with a good 40× objective resolves finer detail than that.

Why do histologists look at a slide at low power before they use a high-power objective?
At 10×, read the width of the field of view in the readout at the bottom right of the view. Change to 40×. How does the width of the field change, and why?
At 40× the field of view is a quarter as wide as at 10× (and covers a sixteenth of the area), because the magnification is four times as great: the same window shows a smaller part of the slide. (The width depends on the size of your window: about 600 µm at 10× in a typical window, and about 150 µm at 40×.)

Measuring and counting

≈ 30 min

How big is it, and how many are there? Calibrate an eyepiece graticule, measure with the microscope’s tools, work with magnifications and scale bars, and count cells with a haemocytometer.

A lot of laboratory work is measurement: how big a cell is, or how many cells there are in a millilitre. In this chapter you calibrate an eyepiece graticule with a stage micrometer, measure with the microscope’s tools, work out sizes from magnifications and scale bars, and count cells with a haemocytometer.

Calibrating an eyepiece graticule

The eyepiece of a microscope can hold a graticule, a glass disc with a scale on it (here, 100 divisions). The graticule is in the eyepiece, not on the slide, so the length on the slide that one division covers depends on the objective. To use it for measuring, you calibrate it against a stage micrometer: a slide with a scale of known length, here 1 mm divided into 100 divisions of 10 µm.

Calibrate the graticule
  1. Open the stage micrometer at 10×.
  2. Switch on the eyepiece view (the eyepiece button in the toolbar). You now see a round field of view, with the graticule across its middle.
  3. The graticule’s divisions (blue) hang down from its line, and the micrometer’s divisions (black) stand up from theirs. Drag the slide sideways so that the 0 of the graticule lines up with a long line of the micrometer. Then look along the two scales for the point, as far along as you can, where a line of the graticule lines up exactly with a line of the micrometer.
At 10×, how many micrometres does one division of the graticule cover?
4 µm. The whole graticule, 100 divisions, lines up with 40 divisions of the micrometer, which is 40 × 10 µm = 400 µm, so one division covers 400 µm ÷ 100 = 4 µm.
Calibrate the other objectives
  1. Change to the 40× objective (6) and line up the scales again.
At 40×, how many micrometres does one division of the graticule cover?
1 µm. The 100 divisions of the graticule now match 10 divisions of the micrometer (100 µm), so one division covers 1 µm: a quarter of what it covered at 10×, because the magnification is four times as great.
One calibration for each objective

What one division of the graticule covers is inversely proportional to the magnification: 4 µm at 10× means 10 µm at 4× and 1 µm at 40×. To measure something, count the divisions across it and multiply by the calibration for that objective.

Measuring with the microscope’s tools

The microscope also has tools that measure for you, using the scale that each slide was scanned at. The Measure tool (the ruler) measures a length, and the Area tool (the outline) measures the area inside an outline you draw. Every measurement is listed in the Measurements panel, and kept in the file microscope/measurements.csv, which R can read.

Measure renal corpuscles
  1. Go back to the kidney , at 10×, and switch the eyepiece view off .
  2. Choose the Measure tool . This button also labels the measurements that follow as “renal corpuscle”, as you can see in the Measurements panel. Measure the diameter of five renal corpuscles: drag across each one at its widest, from the outside of its capsule to the other side.
The Measurements panel shows the mean and standard deviation of your lengths. What is the mean diameter of your renal corpuscles, in micrometres?
Usually between 100 and 200 µm. Renal corpuscles are about 200 µm across in life, but a section cuts most of them away from their widest part, and the tissue shrinks a little as it is processed, so the diameters you measure are smaller and vary.
The renal corpuscles in the section are not all the same size. Give two reasons why.
First, they really do vary in size. Second, a section is a thin slice through the tissue: it cuts through each corpuscle at a different level, and only a slice through the middle shows the full diameter; most slices are nearer the edge and show a smaller circle. (This is why a mean from sections underestimates the true size of round structures.)

Magnification and scale bars

The magnification of a picture is how much bigger it is than the real thing:

magnification = size in the picture ÷ real size   so   real size = size in the picture ÷ magnification

Both sizes have to be in the same units: 1 mm = 1000 µm. A picture that carries a scale bar is safer than a magnification, because the scale bar grows or shrinks with the picture when it is printed or shown at a different size. (The pictures you take with the microscope carry a scale bar.) The calculator is the same as the one in Exam practice.

In a printed micrograph, a scale bar labelled 50 µm is 20 mm long. What is the magnification of the print?
×400. The scale bar is 20 mm = 20 000 µm long in the print and stands for 50 µm, so the magnification is 20 000 µm ÷ 50 µm = 400.
In the same print, a cell is 9 mm wide. How wide is the real cell, in micrometres?
22.5 µm. 9 mm = 9000 µm in the print, and 9000 µm ÷ 400 = 22.5 µm.

Counting cells: the haemocytometer

A haemocytometer is a thick glass slide with a counting chamber of known depth, 0.1 mm, and a grid ruled on its floor. In the commonest kind (the improved Neubauer), the grid has nine large squares, each 1 mm × 1 mm, so the volume above one large square is 1 mm × 1 mm × 0.1 mm = 0.1 mm³ = 0.1 µL = 10−4 mL. You count the cells in several large squares, and work out how many cells there are in a millilitre:

cells per mL = mean number of cells in a large square × 104 × dilution factor

Before counting, the cells are often mixed with trypan blue. Living cells keep the dye out and look clear; dead cells take it up and look blue. To avoid counting a cell twice, count the cells that touch the top or the left-hand line of a square, but not those that touch the bottom or the right-hand line.

Count the cells
  1. Open the haemocytometer . This slide is a drawing, made for practising counts. Find the large square at the top left, which is divided into 16 smaller squares, and look at it at 10×.
  2. Choose the Count tool . This button also names the two categories in the Counts panel: choose a category there, then click on each cell to count it (click a mark again to remove it). Count the living and the dead cells in the top-left large square, going along the rows of small squares so that you don’t miss any.
How many living cells did you count in the top-left large square?
60 living cells (and 8 dead ones). If your count is a cell or two out, look again at the cells that touch the lines of the square.
The four large corner squares held 229 living cells altogether (60 in the top-left square, and 64, 60 and 45 in the other three). The cells had been mixed 1:1 with trypan blue (a dilution factor of 2). How many living cells were there in a millilitre of the original suspension?
1.145 × 106 living cells per mL. The mean is 229 ÷ 4 = 57.25 cells per large square. Each large square holds 10−4 mL, so the suspension in the chamber held 57.25 × 104 = 5.725 × 105 cells per mL; it had been diluted twofold with trypan blue, so the original suspension held 1.145 × 106 cells per mL (about 1.1 × 106).
The same four squares held 26 dead cells. What percentage of the cells were alive (the viability of the cells)?
89.8%. The four squares held 229 + 26 = 255 cells, of which 229 were alive: 229 ÷ 255 × 100 = 89.8%.

Anatomy in 3D

≈ 20 min

The body in 3D, system by system: turn it round, find the organs and structures of physiology, see what lies next to what, and cut through it as a scan does.

The Anatomy tab shows the body in 3D, system by system: the skeleton, the muscles, the heart and the vessels, the organs, the brain and the nerves. Each structure is a separate model that you can select, hide, isolate or cut through, and each has its English and Latin names and a short definition. The models come from Z-Anatomy, an open atlas of anatomy built largely from BodyParts3D, a 3D reconstruction of a man’s body.

Directions in the body

Anatomy describes the body in the anatomical position: standing, facing forwards, arms by the sides with the palms forwards. Anterior is towards the front and posterior towards the back; superior is towards the head and inferior towards the feet; medial is towards the midline and lateral away from it. Left and right are always the body’s own: in a view from the front, the body’s left is on your right, as when you face someone. The small figure at the bottom left of the view shows which way the body faces (S, I, A, P, L and R).

The neurohypophysis is part of the brain, and the adenohypophysis is a gland that grew up from the roof of the mouth. How does the hypothalamus control each of them?
The neurohypophysis is made of the axons of neurons whose cell bodies lie in the hypothalamus: they run down the pituitary stalk and release their hormones (oxytocin and vasopressin, ADH) straight into the blood. The adenohypophysis has no nerves from the hypothalamus: the hypothalamus controls it with releasing and inhibiting hormones, which travel to it in the blood of the hypophysial portal vessels of the stalk.

How many protein chains does haemoglobin have, and how many haem groups?
Four chains (two α and two β globins), each with one haem group: four haem groups, so a molecule of haemoglobin can carry four molecules of oxygen.

The library

The viewer has a library of proteins of physiology and of small molecules – hormones, transmitters and others – ready to open. They are kept with the workbench, so they open quickly.

Open a small molecule
  1. Click Library (the button with a book, at the top left of the viewer) to see what it holds.
  2. Open glucose (the button clears haemoglobin away first). A small molecule is drawn with every atom: here as balls and sticks, coloured by element – grey for carbon, red for oxygen and white for hydrogen.
  3. Open ATP in the same way and find its three phosphate groups (phosphorus is orange).
Why is a protein drawn as a cartoon, while a small molecule is drawn with all its atoms?
A protein has thousands of atoms (haemoglobin has about 4500, not counting hydrogens), so drawing them all gives a tangle in which you cannot see the fold. The cartoon follows the backbone of each chain and shows its helices and sheets. A small molecule has only a few dozen atoms, so all of them can be shown, and its chemistry is in the atoms themselves.

Real recordings of the body

≈ 20 min

The Recorder plays back real recordings of people, as a chart recorder does in a physiology practical – the ECG, heart sounds, blood pressure, breathing, muscle and the brain. Measure intervals, work out rates, and keep your measurements for R.

In a physiology practical, a chart recorder (on a computer, with an amplifier and a data-acquisition unit) records signals from the body – the ECG, the pulse, breathing, blood pressure, muscle – as traces against time. The Recorder tab works the same way, with real recordings of people from PhysioNet, a collection of open databases of physiological signals. Each recording's About panel (click its name above the trace) says what it shows, who it comes from and how to cite it.

The traces of a recording are its channels, one above the other, all on the same time axis. You move along a recording by dragging the trace (or with the strip at the bottom, which shows the whole recording), and zoom with the − and + buttons, or the menu of the time across the screen between them.

Measuring the ECG

Each heartbeat shows in the ECG as a P wave (the atria depolarising), a QRS complex (the ventricles depolarising) and a T wave (the ventricles repolarising). The time from one R wave to the next, the R–R interval, gives the heart rate: heart rate (beats per minute) = 60 ÷ R–R interval (s). The Calipers measure the time between two cursors and work out this rate for you.

Measure a heart rate
  1. Open the recording of sinus rhythm . The upper channel is close to lead II, and the letters above the beats are the labels cardiologists gave them (N: a normal beat).
  2. Zoom in until about 2 seconds fit across the screen . Find the P wave, the QRS complex and the T wave of a beat. At this scale the ECG is drawn on ECG paper: a small square is 0.04 s across and 0.1 mV high.
  3. Choose the Calipers and drag from the peak of one R wave to the peak of the next. The bar above the trace shows the time between the cursors (Δt) and the rate it corresponds to. (You can drag the cursors’ flags, A and B, to put them exactly on the peaks.)
  4. Add the measurement to the data pad with the button at the right of the bar (or press Enter). Measure two more R–R intervals and add them too .
  5. Zoom in further (about 1 s across the screen) and measure the PR interval, from the start of the P wave to the start of the QRS, and the QT interval, from the start of the QRS to the end of the T wave. Add both to the data pad, and type a note beside each in the data pad (PR, QT).
An ECG has an R–R interval of 0.80 s. What is the heart rate, in beats per minute?
75 beats per minute: 60 ÷ 0.80 s = 75. (A normal PR interval is 0.12–0.20 s, mostly the delay in the atrioventricular node; the QT interval is about 0.35–0.45 s, and shortens as the heart rate rises.)

Heart rate and breathing

The Heart rate button adds a channel of the heart rate, worked out beat by beat from the R–R intervals: each dot is one beat.

Watch the heart rate with each breath
  1. Open Heart rate and breathing : an ECG and the breathing of a healthy young woman lying still.
  2. Switch on the heart-rate channel .
  3. Show the whole 90 seconds and compare the heart rate with the breathing trace, which rises as she breathes in.
  4. Press Play : the recording is replayed in real time, as it was recorded, with the pen moving across the chart. Press Stop (or the space bar) to stop.
Does her heart rate rise as she breathes in or as she breathes out? What causes this?
It rises as she breathes in and falls as she breathes out: a respiratory sinus arrhythmia. During inspiration the activity of the vagus nerves to the sinoatrial node falls (the cardiac vagal neurones in the brainstem are inhibited with each inspiration, and the stretch of the lungs and the changing venous return add to this), so the heart speeds up; during expiration the vagal activity returns and the heart slows. It is a normal finding, largest in young, fit people, and it shrinks with age.

The brain: the EEG

Find the alpha rhythm
  1. Open the EEG . The first half was recorded with the eyes open, the second with the eyes closed: the double line marks where one part ends and the other begins. Compare O1, Oz and O2, at the back of the head, before and after it.
  2. Zoom in on the eyes-closed part, with about 1 s across the screen , and use the calipers to measure the time taken by ten alpha waves in O1 (from the peak of one wave to the peak of the eleventh).
Ten alpha waves take 0.95 s. What is the frequency of the alpha rhythm, in hertz (waves a second)?
10.5 Hz: one wave takes 0.95 ÷ 10 = 0.095 s, and 1 ÷ 0.095 s = 10.5 waves a second. The alpha rhythm (8–13 Hz) is strongest over the back of the head with the eyes closed and the person relaxed, and opening the eyes blocks it.

Drugs on an isolated tissue

≈ 25 min

Add drugs to a piece of guinea-pig ileum in an organ bath, build concentration–response curves, see what a competitive antagonist does to them, and analyse them in R.

An organ bath keeps a piece of tissue alive outside the body. A length of guinea-pig ileum, say, hangs in warm physiological salt solution, bubbled with air, and is tied by a thread to a force transducer, which records the tension the tissue develops. Drugs added to the bath reach the tissue’s receptors, and the tension shows how strongly it responds. The Organ bath tab is a simulation of one: each tissue is a little different, its responses take time to develop, and there is noise – but it follows the pharmacology of real tissues.

Setting up

Set up a tissue
  1. Set up a guinea-pig ileum . The drawing shows the bath, and the chart on the right shows the tension against time. The small, irregular movements are the ileum’s own.
  2. The clock runs in real time (×1). Make it run five times faster : the ileum responds within seconds, and the faster clock saves you waiting.

Concentrations in the bath

A drug is added from a stock solution, and the bath dilutes it. If you add a volume v of a stock of concentration Cstock to a bath of volume V, the concentration in the bath is

Cbath = Cstock × v / (V + v)

The tab works this out before you add the drug (under Add a drug). The concentrations of a curve are spaced evenly on a log scale – 10−8, 3 × 10−8, 10−7 M and so on – because the response grows with the logarithm of the concentration over a wide range.

You add 100 µL of a 10−5 M stock of acetylcholine to a 10 mL bath. What is the concentration of acetylcholine in the bath, in M?
9.9 × 10−8 M: 10−5 M × 0.1 mL / (10 mL + 0.1 mL) = 9.9 × 10−8 M, which is close to 10−7 M. (Leaving out the volume you added, 10−5 × 0.1 / 10 = 10−7 M, is only 1% out.)

A concentration–response curve

Each dose is left until the response reaches its plateau, and then washed out; the tissue recovers before the next dose. The tab measures each response – the rise in tension above the baseline before the dose – when you wash, and keeps it in the Results under the chart.

Build a curve for acetylcholine
  1. Add acetylcholine to give 10−8 M in the bath: choose Acetylcholine, a stock of 10−6 M and 100 µL, and press Add to the bath .
  2. Wait for the response to reach its plateau (or press Plateau ), then Wash . The response goes into the Results.
  3. Give the tissue a minute to recover after each wash, and go on up the scale: 3 × 10−8, 10−7, 3 × 10−7, 10−6 and 3 × 10−6 M, each to its plateau and then washed out. Use a stronger stock as you go, to keep the volumes small. When two doses give about the same response, you have reached the maximum.

In the presence of an antagonist

Atropine is an antagonist at muscarinic receptors. To see what it does to the curve, the tissue is equilibrated with a fixed concentration of atropine, and the curve is built again. Here the atropine is put in the solution used for washing, so that its concentration stays the same after every wash.

Repeat the curve in atropine
  1. Put atropine (10−8 M) in the washing solution, and wash the bath with it .
  2. Let it equilibrate with the tissue for 10 minutes: Wait ▸ 10 minutes , or let the clock run at ×20.
  3. Build the curve of acetylcholine again, from 10−7 M up to 10−4 M. In the Results, these responses are marked atropine 1e-8 M.
In atropine, the curve has moved to the right, in parallel, and it reaches the same maximum. What does that tell you about atropine?
What would it mean if, instead, the maximum response fell in the presence of a drug?
That the antagonism is insurmountable: no amount of agonist restores the maximum. The drug might act beyond the receptor – a non-competitive antagonist, such as a relaxant or a Ca²⁺-channel blocker, which stops the muscle contracting whatever the agonist. Or it might inactivate the receptors irreversibly, as phenoxybenzamine does at α-adrenoceptors: with fewer receptors, once the tissue’s spare receptors (its receptor reserve) are used up, even a saturating concentration of agonist can’t produce the full response. (A partial agonist also has a lower maximum, but it causes a response of its own.)

Analysing the curves in R

Fit the curves and work out the pA2
  1. Open the script for your results in RStudio (or press Analyse in R in the tab). It reads the results of your tissue (~/organbath/ileum-1.csv for the first ileum), which the tab keeps up to date.
  2. Run it a line at a time (Ctrl+Enter). It fits a Hill curve to each set of responses with nls() and plots them, and it works out the EC50 of each curve and the dose ratio: how many times more acetylcholine gave the same response in atropine.

For a competitive antagonist the dose ratio (DR) follows the Schild equation, DR = 1 + [B]/KB, where [B] is the antagonist’s concentration and KB its dissociation constant. So each dose ratio gives an estimate of the pA2 = log(DR − 1) − log[B]: the negative log of the concentration of antagonist that makes you double the agonist’s concentration. With several concentrations of antagonist, a Schild plot of log(DR − 1) against log[B] should be a straight line with a slope of 1, which crosses the x-axis where log[B] = −pA2. The script draws one when you have curves at two or more concentrations of atropine.

In one tissue, 10−8 M atropine raised the EC50 of acetylcholine from 8 × 10−8 M to 8.8 × 10−7 M. What is the pA2 of atropine?
9.0. The dose ratio is 8.8 × 10−7 / 8 × 10−8 = 11, so pA2 = log(11 − 1) − log(10−8) = 1 + 8 = 9.0. In other words, 10−9 M (1 nM) atropine doubles the concentration of acetylcholine needed for the same response.

If you have time

Try the other drugs of the ileum. Is the response to histamine blocked by atropine, or by mepyramine? What do drugs X and Y do, and where? (Each tissue has its own; About, ⓘ, tells you when you have decided.) Or set up a ring of rat aorta : contract it with phenylephrine (10−6 M), and then add acetylcholine, which relaxes it through nitric oxide made by the endothelium. Then try a ring whose endothelium has been rubbed off – the experiment with which Furchgott and Zawadzki discovered, in 1980, that the endothelium makes a relaxing factor (nitric oxide).

Flow diagrams that run

≈ 15 min

Draw the boxes-and-arrows diagrams of physiology, and run them to see how the quantities in them change with time.

Physiology is full of flow diagrams: boxes for quantities, such as a concentration or a temperature, and arrows that show what increases (+) or decreases (−) what. In the Diagrams tab you can draw them, and you can also run them. The workbench turns the diagram into a simple model, in which each quantity moves towards what its arrows tell it, and plots how each quantity changes with time. Running a diagram is a good way to check that it does what you think it does.

Kinds of box

A quantity (a rounded box) is something that changes, such as a level or a rate. A stimulus (a flag) pushes on the system from outside: switched on and left on, a pulse, a ramp or a wave. A set point is a fixed level, and a comparator (Σ) adds up its inputs: a + arrow adds and a − arrow subtracts. A green + arrow stimulates, a red − arrow (with a bar at its end) inhibits, and each arrow has a strength and can have a delay.

Run an example

This example is a loop in which a regulated variable is measured, compared with its set point, and corrected by an effector – but the signal from the sensor arrives two minutes late.

A loop with a delay
  1. Open the example . You can also find it in the tab’s Examples menu.
  2. Press Run . The graphs under the diagram show each quantity over the run, and you can drag the slider to see the diagram at any moment. The regulated variable overshoots, and swings up and down, less and less.
  3. Click the arrow labelled sensor. Its settings are in the panel on the right: change its Delay to 0.5 min, and press Run again.
  4. Now make the delay 4 min, and run it again.
What happens to the swings when the delay is short, and when it is long? Why does a delay in a loop like this make it swing?
With a short delay the variable settles quickly, with little or no overshoot; with a long delay the swings get bigger and bigger. The effector corrects for the error that the sensor reported, but with a delay that report is out of date: the effector keeps pushing after the error has gone, and overshoots, and then over-corrects the other way. The longer the delay (or the stronger the correction), the bigger each overshoot.

Draw your own

Build a small loop
  1. Start a blank diagram , click its name at the top, and call it My loop.
  2. Add a stimulus with Box ▸ A stimulus or disturbance, and two quantities by double-clicking on empty parts of the diagram. Double-click each box to name it: Push (the stimulus), Level and Response.
  3. Draw a + arrow from Push to Level: drag from one of the dots on the edge of Push to Level. Draw another + arrow from Level to Response.
  4. Choose − in the toolbar and draw an arrow from Response back to Level, which closes the loop.
  5. Run your diagram, and look at the level of Level at the end (the Numbers under the diagram).
  6. Right-click the − arrow and block it, and run the diagram again.
  7. Save the diagram (Save, in your diagrams folder), and keep the results of the run as a table for R (Results to my files).
How did blocking the − arrow change what happened to Level, and why?
Without the loop, Level rose by the whole size of the push (from 1 to 2, with the settings a new box starts with). With the loop it rose only half as far (to 1.5), because Response rose with it and pushed it back down: the − arrow opposes the change.
Diagrams in your work

The Export menu saves a diagram as a picture (PNG or SVG) for a report, and the results of a run as a CSV table that you can plot in RStudio. A diagram saved in your files opens again in this tab when you click on it in Files.

Ohm’s law

The current through a resistor is proportional to the voltage across it: I = V / R.

Check Ohm’s law
  1. Open the example : a 9 V battery, a 1 kΩ resistor, an ammeter in series and a voltmeter in parallel with the resistor.
  2. Press Run . The moving dots show the current, and the meters show their readings.
What current does the ammeter show, in milliamps (mA)?
9 mA: I = V / R = 9 V / 1000 Ω = 0.009 A.
Change the resistance

What current flows through a 2.2 kΩ resistor, in mA?
4.09 mA: 9 V / 2200 Ω = 0.00409 A. Doubling the resistance (roughly) halves the current.

Charging a capacitor

Interactive simulations

≈ 15 min

Models of the science behind the body that you can change and watch: the ions of an action potential, the light a solution absorbs, and the logarithmic scale of pH.

The Simulations tab holds a library of simulations made by PhET Interactive Simulations at the University of Colorado Boulder, chosen for biomedical science. Each one is a model of a piece of science – ions crossing a membrane, light passing through a solution, the particles of a gas – that you can change and watch. Each has its own controls, and many have several screens, which you choose from the bar at the bottom of the simulation (or from the list in the tab’s toolbar). The ⓘ button tells you what a simulation is for, who made it and its licence.

The action potential

The Neuron simulation shows a short length of an axon, cut across, with the channels in its membrane and the sodium and potassium ions on either side.

Stimulate a neuron
  1. Look through the library of the Simulations tab, and choose the topic Membranes and nerves to see the simulations about membranes.
  2. Open the Neuron simulation . The legend on the right shows the ions and the four kinds of channel.
  3. Press Stimulate Neuron and watch the channels and the ions. Choose Slow Motion, or pause and step it with the buttons under the axon, to see the order of events.
  4. Tick Potential Chart (under Show) and stimulate the neuron again: the chart plots the membrane potential against time – the action potential.
  5. Tick Concentrations as well, and stimulate it once more, watching the concentrations of Na⁺ and K⁺ inside and outside the axon.
When the neuron is stimulated, which channels open first, and which way do the ions move through them?
The voltage-gated sodium channels open first, and Na⁺ moves into the axon: the rising phase of the action potential. Then the sodium channels close (they inactivate) and the voltage-gated potassium channels open, K⁺ moves out, and the potential falls again – briefly below the resting potential, until the potassium channels close.
Did the concentrations of Na⁺ and K⁺ inside the axon change much during an action potential? What does that tell you?
Hardly at all. Only a tiny fraction of the ions cross the membrane in one action potential: enough to change the charge on the membrane, which (like a capacitor) needs little charge to change its voltage, but far too few to change the concentrations. That is why a neuron can fire many action potentials before the Na⁺/K⁺ pump has to restore the gradients.

Absorbance and concentration

A spectrophotometer measures how much light of one wavelength a solution absorbs. The Beer–Lambert law says that the absorbance, A, is proportional to the concentration, c, and to the distance the light travels through the solution, the path length, l:

A = ε c l

where ε, the molar absorptivity (or extinction coefficient), depends on the substance and on the wavelength. It is why a standard curve of absorbance against concentration is a straight line, and why cuvettes have a standard width of 1 cm.

Measure absorbance
  1. Open Beer’s Law Lab on its Beer’s Law screen .
  2. Switch the light on (the red button), choose Absorbance on the detector, and drag the detector’s probe into the beam behind the cuvette.
  3. Change the concentration of the drink mix with its slider, and watch the absorbance: double the concentration, and the absorbance doubles.
  4. Change the path length: drag the yellow arrows on the side of the cuvette to make it wider or narrower, and measure it with the ruler.
With the drink mix at 100 mM in a 1.00 cm cuvette, the absorbance at 508 nm is 0.51. What absorbance do you expect from a 50 mM solution in a 2.00 cm cuvette? Check your answer in the simulation.
0.51, the same: halving the concentration halves the absorbance, and doubling the path length doubles it. In A = εcl, (½c)(2l) = cl.

pH, a logarithmic scale

The pH of a solution is minus the logarithm (to base 10) of the concentration of hydrogen ions – strictly, H₃O⁺ – in moles per litre:

pH = −log10 [H₃O⁺]

so each step of one pH unit is a tenfold change in the concentration of H₃O⁺.

The pH of blood
  1. Open pH Scale on its Micro screen , which shows the concentrations of H₃O⁺ and OH⁻ as well as the pH.
  2. Choose Blood from the list of liquids at the top. Read its pH, and the concentration of H₃O⁺ on the graph on the left (the graph shows concentrations when its switch is set to Concentration).
  3. Add water from the tap at the top right, and watch the pH and the concentrations as the blood is diluted.
Blood has a pH of 7.4. What is the concentration of H₃O⁺ in blood, in nanomoles per litre?
About 40 nmol L⁻¹: 10−7.4 mol L⁻¹ = 4.0 × 10−8 mol L⁻¹ = 40 nmol L⁻¹. A fall in pH of only 0.3, to 7.1, doubles it (100.3 ≈ 2).
A model is not the body

In the simulation, adding water to blood moves its pH towards 7, as it would for any solution of an acid or a base. Real blood resists such changes: its buffers – bicarbonate above all, with proteins such as haemoglobin, and phosphate – take up or release H⁺, and the lungs and kidneys adjust them. A simulation shows what its model predicts, and its model is simpler than the body. Use simulations to build a picture and to test your reasoning, and keep in mind what they leave out.

Calculations in a script

A calculation written as a script is easy to check, and easy to run again with new numbers. This script does the haemocytometer calculation of the last chapter.

scripts/haemocytometer.R
# Counting cells with a haemocytometer
# the living (clear) and dead (blue) cells in the four large corner squares
live <- c(60, 64, 60, 45)
dead <- c(8, 8, 8, 2)

volume_mL <- 1e-4   # the volume over one large square: 1 mm x 1 mm x 0.1 mm = 0.1 uL = 0.0001 mL
dilution <- 2       # the cells were mixed 1:1 with trypan blue

# living cells per mL of the original suspension
cells_per_mL <- mean(live) / volume_mL * dilution
cells_per_mL
format(cells_per_mL, scientific = TRUE)

# the percentage of the cells that are alive (the viability)
viability <- 100 * sum(live) / (sum(live) + sum(dead))
round(viability, 1)
Create this file saves the script in your files and opens it in RStudio.
Run the calculation
  1. Click Create this file above.
  2. Press Ctrl+Shift+Enter (⌘+Shift+Enter on a Mac), or choose Source with Echo from the arrow beside the Source button: R runs the whole script and shows each line and its result in the Console. (The Source button itself runs the script without showing the results.) Check that R gets the same answers as you did.
  3. Change the counts to live <- c(48, 52, 50, 46), save the script (Ctrl+S) and run it again.
With the new counts (48, 52, 50 and 46 living cells, and the same dead cells), how many living cells were there per mL of the original suspension?
9.8 × 105 cells per mL: the mean is 196 ÷ 4 = 49 cells per large square, and 49 ÷ 10−4 mL × 2 = 980 000 cells per mL.
