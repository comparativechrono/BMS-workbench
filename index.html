The BMS workbench

≈ 10 min

A virtual microscope, flow diagrams and circuits that you can run, exam practice, molecules in 3D and R for your data – the tools of your practicals, in one web page.

What’s in the workbench

The workbench on the right brings together the tools of a biomedical science practical. Each tab is a different tool:

MicroscopeA virtual microscope with slides of human tissues: objectives from 1× to 40×, an eyepiece graticule, and tools to measure, count, label and take pictures.
DiagramsFlow diagrams of how the body controls itself – boxes, and arrows that increase (+) or decrease (−) – which you can run to see how things change with time.
CircuitsElectrical circuits, and the circuit models of physiology, built and run as on a lab bench, with meters and traces.
Exam practiceAn essay editor that works like the online exam system: a timer, a word count, a calculator, flags, and downloads as Word documents.
3D viewerProteins and smaller molecules in 3D, with a library of molecules of physiology.
RStudioR, for analysing data – including the measurements and counts that you make with the microscope.
FilesEverything you save: measurements, counts, pictures, diagrams, circuits, essays and R scripts.

The tools work together. The microscope keeps your measurements and counts in files that R can read, and your pictures in Files; you can run a diagram or a circuit and keep its results; and the essays you write in Exam practice can be downloaded as Word documents. This short practical takes you round each of the tools, so that you know your way around before you use them in the practicals of the course.

Looking at a slide

≈ 25 min

The Microscope tab works like a light microscope: a slide box, objectives from 1× to 40× and a stage you move by dragging. In this chapter you look at the kidney and the liver, find structures, label them and keep pictures.

Its objectives work as they do in digital microscopy: at 20× you see a slide at the full resolution of its scan, and 10× shows it half as big, 40× twice as big. The slides are sections of human tissues (normal, apart from one coronary artery with atherosclerosis), stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and scanned at high resolution, so you can look at a whole section at low power and then zoom in on single cells. Haematoxylin stains nuclei blue-purple and eosin stains cytoplasm and collagen pink.

From low power to high power

Always start with a low-power objective, which shows you the whole section, so that you can see how the tissue is organised and choose where to look. Then move up through the objectives. The readout at the bottom right of the view shows the magnification and the size of the field of view, and the map at the top right shows which part of the slide you are looking at.

Look at the kidney
  1. Open the slide of the kidney . You are looking at the outer part of the kidney, the cortex.
  2. Click on the name of the slide, above the view, to read about it: how it was stained, how big it is, what to look for, and where it comes from.
  3. Click the 10× objective (or press 4, as the objectives are numbered 1 to 6). Drag the slide to move round it, and scroll (or pinch) to zoom in and out.
  4. Now look at part of the slide with the 40× objective (6).
Magnification, resolution and the field of view

Through a real microscope, the total magnification is that of the objective multiplied by that of the eyepiece (usually 10×): a 40× objective gives 400×. As the magnification goes up, the field of view gets smaller: at 40× you see a quarter of the width of the field at 10×. (On a screen, how much you see also depends on the size of your window.) What limits the detail you can see is the resolution, the smallest distance between two points that can still be told apart. These slides were scanned with a 20× objective, about 0.5 µm per pixel, so the 40× objective makes the picture bigger without showing more detail. A real microscope with a good 40× objective resolves finer detail than that.

Why do histologists look at a slide at low power before they use a high-power objective?
At 10×, read the width of the field of view in the readout at the bottom right of the view. Change to 40×. How does the width of the field change, and why?
At 40× the field of view is a quarter as wide as at 10× (and covers a sixteenth of the area), because the magnification is four times as great: the same window shows a smaller part of the slide. (The width depends on the size of your window: about 600 µm at 10× in a typical window, and about 150 µm at 40×.)

How could you tell a central vein from a portal tract at low power?
A central vein is a single thin-walled vessel with liver cells right up to its wall, and the plates of liver cells run out from it like spokes. A portal tract is a patch of pink connective tissue that holds several tubes: a branch of the portal vein, a small artery and one or more bile ducts.

Measuring and counting

≈ 30 min

How big is it, and how many are there? Calibrate an eyepiece graticule, measure with the microscope’s tools, work with magnifications and scale bars, and count cells with a haemocytometer.

A lot of laboratory work is measurement: how big a cell is, or how many cells there are in a millilitre. In this chapter you calibrate an eyepiece graticule with a stage micrometer, measure with the microscope’s tools, work out sizes from magnifications and scale bars, and count cells with a haemocytometer.

Calibrating an eyepiece graticule

The eyepiece of a microscope can hold a graticule, a glass disc with a scale on it (here, 100 divisions). The graticule is in the eyepiece, not on the slide, so the length on the slide that one division covers depends on the objective. To use it for measuring, you calibrate it against a stage micrometer: a slide with a scale of known length, here 1 mm divided into 100 divisions of 10 µm.

Calibrate the graticule
  1. Open the stage micrometer at 10×.
  2. Switch on the eyepiece view (the eyepiece button in the toolbar). You now see a round field of view, with the graticule across its middle.
  3. The graticule’s divisions (blue) hang down from its line, and the micrometer’s divisions (black) stand up from theirs. Drag the slide sideways so that the 0 of the graticule lines up with a long line of the micrometer. Then look along the two scales for the point, as far along as you can, where a line of the graticule lines up exactly with a line of the micrometer.
At 10×, how many micrometres does one division of the graticule cover?
4 µm. The whole graticule, 100 divisions, lines up with 40 divisions of the micrometer, which is 40 × 10 µm = 400 µm, so one division covers 400 µm ÷ 100 = 4 µm.
Calibrate the other objectives
  1. Change to the 40× objective (6) and line up the scales again.
At 40×, how many micrometres does one division of the graticule cover?
1 µm. The 100 divisions of the graticule now match 10 divisions of the micrometer (100 µm), so one division covers 1 µm: a quarter of what it covered at 10×, because the magnification is four times as great.
One calibration for each objective

What one division of the graticule covers is inversely proportional to the magnification: 4 µm at 10× means 10 µm at 4× and 1 µm at 40×. To measure something, count the divisions across it and multiply by the calibration for that objective.

Measuring with the microscope’s tools

The microscope also has tools that measure for you, using the scale that each slide was scanned at. The Measure tool (the ruler) measures a length, and the Area tool (the outline) measures the area inside an outline you draw. Every measurement is listed in the Measurements panel, and kept in the file microscope/measurements.csv, which R can read.

Measure renal corpuscles
  1. Go back to the kidney , at 10×, and switch the eyepiece view off .
  2. Choose the Measure tool . This button also labels the measurements that follow as “renal corpuscle”, as you can see in the Measurements panel. Measure the diameter of five renal corpuscles: drag across each one at its widest, from the outside of its capsule to the other side.
The Measurements panel shows the mean and standard deviation of your lengths. What is the mean diameter of your renal corpuscles, in micrometres?
Usually between 100 and 200 µm. Renal corpuscles are about 200 µm across in life, but a section cuts most of them away from their widest part, and the tissue shrinks a little as it is processed, so the diameters you measure are smaller and vary.
The renal corpuscles in the section are not all the same size. Give two reasons why.
First, they really do vary in size. Second, a section is a thin slice through the tissue: it cuts through each corpuscle at a different level, and only a slice through the middle shows the full diameter; most slices are nearer the edge and show a smaller circle. (This is why a mean from sections underestimates the true size of round structures.)

Magnification and scale bars

The magnification of a picture is how much bigger it is than the real thing:

magnification = size in the picture ÷ real size   so   real size = size in the picture ÷ magnification

Both sizes have to be in the same units: 1 mm = 1000 µm. A picture that carries a scale bar is safer than a magnification, because the scale bar grows or shrinks with the picture when it is printed or shown at a different size. (The pictures you take with the microscope carry a scale bar.) The calculator is the same as the one in Exam practice.

In a printed micrograph, a scale bar labelled 50 µm is 20 mm long. What is the magnification of the print?
×400. The scale bar is 20 mm = 20 000 µm long in the print and stands for 50 µm, so the magnification is 20 000 µm ÷ 50 µm = 400.
In the same print, a cell is 9 mm wide. How wide is the real cell, in micrometres?
22.5 µm. 9 mm = 9000 µm in the print, and 9000 µm ÷ 400 = 22.5 µm.

Counting cells: the haemocytometer

A haemocytometer is a thick glass slide with a counting chamber of known depth, 0.1 mm, and a grid ruled on its floor. In the commonest kind (the improved Neubauer), the grid has nine large squares, each 1 mm × 1 mm, so the volume above one large square is 1 mm × 1 mm × 0.1 mm = 0.1 mm³ = 0.1 µL = 10−4 mL. You count the cells in several large squares, and work out how many cells there are in a millilitre:

cells per mL = mean number of cells in a large square × 104 × dilution factor

Before counting, the cells are often mixed with trypan blue. Living cells keep the dye out and look clear; dead cells take it up and look blue. To avoid counting a cell twice, count the cells that touch the top or the left-hand line of a square, but not those that touch the bottom or the right-hand line.

Count the cells
  1. Open the haemocytometer . This slide is a drawing, made for practising counts. Find the large square at the top left, which is divided into 16 smaller squares, and look at it at 10×.
  2. Choose the Count tool . This button also names the two categories in the Counts panel: choose a category there, then click on each cell to count it (click a mark again to remove it). Count the living and the dead cells in the top-left large square, going along the rows of small squares so that you don’t miss any.
How many living cells did you count in the top-left large square?
60 living cells (and 8 dead ones). If your count is a cell or two out, look again at the cells that touch the lines of the square.
The four large corner squares held 229 living cells altogether (60 in the top-left square, and 64, 60 and 45 in the other three). The cells had been mixed 1:1 with trypan blue (a dilution factor of 2). How many living cells were there in a millilitre of the original suspension?
1.145 × 106 living cells per mL. The mean is 229 ÷ 4 = 57.25 cells per large square. Each large square holds 10−4 mL, so the suspension in the chamber held 57.25 × 104 = 5.725 × 105 cells per mL; it had been diluted twofold with trypan blue, so the original suspension held 1.145 × 106 cells per mL (about 1.1 × 106).
The same four squares held 26 dead cells. What percentage of the cells were alive (the viability of the cells)?
89.8%. The four squares held 229 + 26 = 255 cells, of which 229 were alive: 229 ÷ 255 × 100 = 89.8%.
Your counts in R

The marks you make with the Count tool are kept in microscope/counts.csv, with the slide, the category and where each mark is. In the last chapter you read your measurements and counts in R, and do this calculation there too.

Flow diagrams that run

≈ 15 min

Draw the boxes-and-arrows diagrams of physiology, and run them to see how the quantities in them change with time.

Physiology is full of flow diagrams: boxes for quantities, such as a concentration or a temperature, and arrows that show what increases (+) or decreases (−) what. In the Diagrams tab you can draw them, and you can also run them. The workbench turns the diagram into a simple model, in which each quantity moves towards what its arrows tell it, and plots how each quantity changes with time. Running a diagram is a good way to check that it does what you think it does.

Kinds of box

A quantity (a rounded box) is something that changes, such as a level or a rate. A stimulus (a flag) pushes on the system from outside: switched on and left on, a pulse, a ramp or a wave. A set point is a fixed level, and a comparator (Σ) adds up its inputs: a + arrow adds and a − arrow subtracts. A green + arrow stimulates, a red − arrow (with a bar at its end) inhibits, and each arrow has a strength and can have a delay.

Run an example

This example is a loop in which a regulated variable is measured, compared with its set point, and corrected by an effector – but the signal from the sensor arrives two minutes late.

A loop with a delay
  1. Open the example . You can also find it in the tab’s Examples menu.
  2. Press Run . The graphs under the diagram show each quantity over the run, and you can drag the slider to see the diagram at any moment. The regulated variable overshoots, and swings up and down, less and less.
  3. Click the arrow labelled sensor. Its settings are in the panel on the right: change its Delay to 0.5 min, and press Run again.
  4. Now make the delay 4 min, and run it again.
What happens to the swings when the delay is short, and when it is long? Why does a delay in a loop like this make it swing?
With a short delay the variable settles quickly, with little or no overshoot; with a long delay the swings get bigger and bigger. The effector corrects for the error that the sensor reported, but with a delay that report is out of date: the effector keeps pushing after the error has gone, and overshoots, and then over-corrects the other way. The longer the delay (or the stronger the correction), the bigger each overshoot.

Draw your own

Build a small loop
  1. Start a blank diagram , click its name at the top, and call it My loop.
  2. Add a stimulus with Box ▸ A stimulus or disturbance, and two quantities by double-clicking on empty parts of the diagram. Double-click each box to name it: Push (the stimulus), Level and Response.
  3. Draw a + arrow from Push to Level: drag from one of the dots on the edge of Push to Level. Draw another + arrow from Level to Response.
  4. Choose − in the toolbar and draw an arrow from Response back to Level, which closes the loop.
  5. Run your diagram, and look at the level of Level at the end (the Numbers under the diagram).
  6. Right-click the − arrow and block it, and run the diagram again.
  7. Save the diagram (Save, in your diagrams folder), and keep the results of the run as a table for R (Results to my files).
How did blocking the − arrow change what happened to Level, and why?
Without the loop, Level rose by the whole size of the push (from 1 to 2, with the settings a new box starts with). With the loop it rose only half as far (to 1.5), because Response rose with it and pushed it back down: the − arrow opposes the change.
Diagrams in your work

The Export menu saves a diagram as a picture (PNG or SVG) for a report, and the results of a run as a CSV table that you can plot in RStudio. A diagram saved in your files opens again in this tab when you click on it in Files.

Circuits on the bench

≈ 15 min

Build electrical circuits and run them, and use the same circuits as models of the body.

The Circuits tab is an electronics bench: batteries, resistors, capacitors, switches and meters, joined by wires, and a simulator that works out the voltage at every point and the current through every part as time passes. In its Physiology mode the same circuits become models of the body: pressure for voltage, flow for current, resistance to flow for resistance, and compliance for capacitance.

Ohm’s law

The current through a resistor is proportional to the voltage across it: I = V / R.

Check Ohm’s law
  1. Open the example : a 9 V battery, a 1 kΩ resistor, an ammeter in series and a voltmeter in parallel with the resistor.
  2. Press Run . The moving dots show the current, and the meters show their readings.
What current does the ammeter show, in milliamps (mA)?
9 mA: I = V / R = 9 V / 1000 Ω = 0.009 A.
Change the resistance
  1. Click the resistor and change its resistance to 2.2k (2.2 kΩ) in the panel on the right.
What current flows through a 2.2 kΩ resistor, in mA?
4.09 mA: 9 V / 2200 Ω = 0.00409 A. Doubling the resistance (roughly) halves the current.

Charging a capacitor

A capacitor stores charge. Charged through a resistor, it charges quickly at first and then more and more slowly: after one time constant, τ = RC, it has reached 63% of its final voltage.

Watch a capacitor charge
  1. Open the example and press Run.
  2. Click the switch labelled Charge to close it, and watch the trace of the capacitor’s voltage under the circuit. Then open it again, and close Discharge.
With R1 = 10 kΩ and C1 = 100 µF, how long does the capacitor take to charge to 63% of the battery’s 5 V (3.16 V), in seconds?
1 s: τ = RC = 10 000 Ω × 0.0001 F = 1 s. After 2τ it has reached 86%, and after 3τ 95%.

Writing answers as in the exam

≈ 20 min

Practise writing essays in the browser, as you will in the exam: a timer, a word count, the editor’s toolbar, flags, the calculator, and a Word document of your answers at the end.

Your exams are written on a computer, in an online exam system (Inspera). The Exam practice tab works in the same way, so that you can get used to planning, writing and checking an essay in a browser – with a timer and a word limit – before it matters. Your practicals will set essays here too.

What is the same, and what isn’t

The layout, the timer, the editor and its toolbar, flags, the calculator and the submission page work as they do in the exam system. As in the exam browser, you can copy and paste within the exam, but text copied from anywhere else can’t be pasted in – so write your answers here, not in another program. But this is practice: your answers are saved only in this browser, nobody marks them, and after you submit you can review them alongside the marking guidance and download them as a Word document.

What is one thing about writing in the browser that is different from writing by hand, and how will you allow for it in an exam?
For example: you can edit and move text, so it is worth writing a plan (headings, or a list that you delete later) and then filling it in; the word count shows how much you have written, so you can share your time between the sections; there is no spelling checker in most exams, so leave time to read your answer through; drawings take longer than on paper, so keep them simple; and you can’t see the whole essay at once, so a clear structure (paragraphs with a point each) matters even more.

Molecules in 3D

≈ 10 min

The structures of proteins and of the small molecules of physiology, in 3D: open them, turn them round and colour them.

The 3D viewer shows the structures of proteins and smaller molecules: where each atom is, as worked out (mostly) by X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy, and kept in the Protein Data Bank (PDB). You can turn a molecule round, change how it is drawn and coloured, and look closely at the parts that matter, such as the site where something binds.

A protein: haemoglobin

Look at haemoglobin
  1. Open haemoglobin (its PDB ID is 4HHB). It is drawn as a cartoon, which follows the backbone of each chain: the coils are α helices.
  2. Drag to turn the molecule round, scroll to zoom, and right-drag (or two-finger drag) to move it.
  3. Colour each chain differently .
  4. Show the haem groups as spheres , coloured by element . The iron atom at the centre of each haem is where oxygen binds.
How many protein chains does haemoglobin have, and how many haem groups?
Four chains (two α and two β globins), each with one haem group: four haem groups, so a molecule of haemoglobin can carry four molecules of oxygen.

The library

The viewer has a library of proteins of physiology and of small molecules – hormones, transmitters and others – ready to open. They are kept with the workbench, so they open quickly.

Open a small molecule
  1. Click Library (the button with a book, at the top left of the viewer) to see what it holds.
  2. Open glucose (the button clears haemoglobin away first). A small molecule is drawn with every atom: here as balls and sticks, coloured by element – grey for carbon, red for oxygen and white for hydrogen.
  3. Open ATP in the same way and find its three phosphate groups (phosphorus is orange).
Why is a protein drawn as a cartoon, while a small molecule is drawn with all its atoms?
A protein has thousands of atoms (haemoglobin has about 4500, not counting hydrogens), so drawing them all gives a tangle in which you cannot see the fold. The cartoon follows the backbone of each chain and shows its helices and sheets. A small molecule has only a few dozen atoms, so all of them can be shown, and its chemistry is in the atoms themselves.

From the microscope to R

≈ 15 min

Your measurements and counts are kept in files that R can read. Summarise and plot them in RStudio, and turn a calculation into a script.

The microscope keeps your measurements and counts in two files in your microscope folder, measurements.csv and counts.csv. Each row is one measurement (or one mark of the Count tool), so they are ready for R. In this chapter you read them in RStudio, summarise them and plot them. You only need to run the scripts – but have a look at each line, to see what it does.

The first time

The first time you open RStudio, the workbench downloads R itself (about 30 MB), which can take a minute. After that, the browser keeps it.

Your measurements

Summarise your measurements
  1. Look at your measurements in Files . Each row has the slide, what you measured (its label), the value and its unit, the objective, and where on the slide it was.
  2. Open the microscope’s script for your measurements in RStudio . (In the microscope, the Analyse in R button of the Measurements panel does the same.)
  3. Put the cursor on the first line of code and press Ctrl+Enter (⌘+Enter on a Mac) to run it. Keep pressing it to run the script line by line, and watch the Console.
  4. When you reach boxplot(…), the box plot of your lengths appears in the Plots pane.
R gives the mean, the standard deviation (SD) and the number of your renal corpuscle measurements. What does the standard deviation tell you?
How spread out the measurements are around the mean: roughly, how far a typical measurement lies from the mean. About two thirds of measurements from a normal distribution lie within one SD of the mean. A large SD compared with the mean means that the diameters varied a lot (here, because the corpuscles are cut at different levels).

Your counts

Count your counts
  1. Open the script for your counts and run it line by line. table() counts the marks of each category on each slide, and prop.table() turns the counts into proportions.

Calculations in a script

A calculation written as a script is easy to check, and easy to run again with new numbers. This script does the haemocytometer calculation of the last chapter.

scripts/haemocytometer.R
# Counting cells with a haemocytometer
# the living (clear) and dead (blue) cells in the four large corner squares
live <- c(60, 64, 60, 45)
dead <- c(8, 8, 8, 2)

volume_mL <- 1e-4   # the volume over one large square: 1 mm x 1 mm x 0.1 mm = 0.1 uL = 0.0001 mL
dilution <- 2       # the cells were mixed 1:1 with trypan blue

# living cells per mL of the original suspension
cells_per_mL <- mean(live) / volume_mL * dilution
cells_per_mL
format(cells_per_mL, scientific = TRUE)

# the percentage of the cells that are alive (the viability)
viability <- 100 * sum(live) / (sum(live) + sum(dead))
round(viability, 1)
Create this file saves the script in your files and opens it in RStudio.
Run the calculation
  1. Click Create this file above.
  2. Press Ctrl+Shift+Enter (⌘+Shift+Enter on a Mac), or choose Source with Echo from the arrow beside the Source button: R runs the whole script and shows each line and its result in the Console. (The Source button itself runs the script without showing the results.) Check that R gets the same answers as you did.
  3. Change the counts to live <- c(48, 52, 50, 46), save the script (Ctrl+S) and run it again.
With the new counts (48, 52, 50 and 46 living cells, and the same dead cells), how many living cells were there per mL of the original suspension?
9.8 × 105 cells per mL: the mean is 196 ÷ 4 = 49 cells per large square, and 49 ÷ 10−4 mL × 2 = 980 000 cells per mL.
